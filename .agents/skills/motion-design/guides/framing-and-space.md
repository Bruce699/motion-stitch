# Framing and space

Use this guide to choose what the viewer can see and how the view changes. First decide what the next framing contributes: inspection, scale, orientation, depth, comparison or atmosphere.

## Choose the view

| View | Useful for | Common failure |
|---|---|---|
| Frontal | Reading UI, comparing shape, showing an interaction | Treating every scene as a complete centered screenshot |
| Macro or crop | Inspecting a small material, contour or alignment | Enlarging a detail that has no significance in the brief |
| Oblique perspective | Showing depth, thickness and spatial relationships | Text becomes unreadable at the beat where it matters |
| Orthographic / isometric | Parallel relationships, exploded structures, diagrammatic compositions | Calling any angled view isometric or hiding overlap behind the chosen angle |
| Quiet wide view | Context, family, sustained local action | Too much empty time or insufficient detail for the intended action |

Frontal describes orientation, not projection. An oblique view alone does not prove perspective or isometric projection. When authoring an exact projection, use the host's actual camera settings. When analyzing footage, qualify what cannot be established.

## Choose whether the view needs to move

- **Stable view with local action:** useful when the interface or object already supplies the event.
- **Push toward a detail:** choose the destination first; preserve enough context for the target to remain recognizable.
- **Widen to context:** reveal a meaningful containing object, layout or family.
- **Survey:** travel across related details at a useful inspection scale. A lateral move can compare without repeated zooms.
- **Relative rotation:** expose another face, edge or layer relationship. The chosen start and end views should each serve the composition.
- **Restrained drift:** sustain inspection or atmosphere when small changes in contour or light matter.
- **Cut to another view:** select two useful framings without requiring a continuous journey between them.

Camera movement and object movement can produce similar screen results. Choose a controllable implementation in authored work; do not claim an original orbit rig from ambiguous footage.

## Coordinate with the subject and next scene

Keep global framing and internal action legible as distinct contributions. A camera can follow a subject, reveal its surroundings or move against it, but none is a universal rule. Watch whether the important part moves too fast across the screen when both motions combine.

Check the last framing of one scene against the first useful framing of the next: focal location, scale, direction, density and projection. Continuity can carry attention; a deliberate contrast can mark a new idea. Avoid accidental repeated push-ins simply because each looks appealing alone.

Verify the tightest crop and most oblique view at output resolution. Use a readable orientation when reading is required; expressive blur, crops and partial views remain valid elsewhere. A continuous orbit, shallow focus and constant movement are optional choices, not requirements for cinematic work.
